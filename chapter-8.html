1Core

Modes as home keys

Chapter 3 treated modes as scales to play over a chord. A mode can also be a key: a piece can live in D Dorian the way it can live in C major. Two things make that work. The mode's first chord has to feel like home, and the progression has to feature the chord containing the mode's characteristic note, the one note that separates it from plain major or minor.

Mode
Root

Avoid the pull of the parent key. D Dorian uses the notes of C major, and a G7 chord would drag the ear straight to C. Modal pieces keep to two or three chords and keep returning to the home chord for that reason.

Brighter and darker on one root

A piece can change mode without changing its home note, for example from C Ionian to C Mixolydian. Because each mode in the bright-to-dark order differs from its neighbour by one note, moving one step along the order changes one note and shifts the mood a shade. Moving several steps at once is a bigger jolt.

Root
Bright → dark

Mirror pairs

A curiosity that explains the order: write a mode's pattern of whole and half steps backwards and you get another mode. The pairs sit opposite each other in the bright-to-dark list.

Scales from melodic and harmonic minor

The melodic minor scale has seven modes of its own, and three of them are everyday tools in jazz and fusion. Harmonic minor contributes one more. Each is best learned as "the scale for one kind of chord".

Altered dominant chords

A dominant 7 chord is already tense. Raising or lowering its 9th, 11th or 13th adds notes that sit a half step from the chord you are resolving to, so the release is stronger. The four alterations are ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 and ♭13; "alt" means some mixture of them.

Dominant on

▶ plays the altered chord and then the major chord it resolves to, a 5th below.

Triads on top: upper structures

An easy way to play and hear those altered sounds is to think of two simple things at once: the shell of the dominant chord underneath (root, 3rd, ♭7) and an ordinary major triad on top. Each triad supplies a different set of tensions.

Chapter 8 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Play a two-chord vamp in Dorian and in Mixolydian and name the characteristic note of each.
  • Explain why a modal progression avoids the V7 of its parent key.
  • Name one scale each for a maj7, m7, 7 and m7♭5 chord without looking.
  • Play one altered dominant resolving to its I chord.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Three minutes: loop a Dorian vamp and improvise, leaning on the natural 6th.
  2. Three minutes: the same with a Mixolydian vamp and its ♭7.
  3. Two minutes: V7 to I in one key, then V7♭9 to I, then V7alt to I. Listen to the pull increase.
  4. Two minutes: one upper-structure triad over a dominant shell, resolved to I.
