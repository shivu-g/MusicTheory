1Core

The twelve notes

Western music uses only twelve different notes. Seven have plain letter names, A to G, called natural notes. The other five sit in the gaps between them and each has two names: a sharp (♯, one step higher than the letter below) or a flat (♭, one step lower than the letter above). C♯ and D♭ are the same pitch.

The smallest distance between two notes is a half step: one fret on the guitar, or the very next key on the piano. Two half steps make a whole step: two frets. After twelve half steps you arrive at the same letter again, one octave higher.

All twelve, then back to C

Dark chips are the five in-between notes. Red markers show the two places with no in-between note.

On piano the pattern is visible: white keys are naturals, black keys are sharps and flats. There is no black key between E and F or between B and C.

The same twelve notes along the low E string. Each fret is one half step, and fret 12 is the octave.

Memorise this first. E to F and B to C are half steps (one fret). Every other pair of neighbouring letters is a whole step (two frets). Everything on the neck follows from that.
2Core

How the guitar is tuned

The six open strings, from thickest to thinnest, are E A D G B E. Each string is tuned five frets above the one before it, with one exception: the B string is only four frets above the G string. That single irregularity is why chord and scale shapes change when they reach the top two strings, and it comes up in almost every lesson that follows.

The 5th-fret rule: fret 5 on a string sounds the same as the next open string. On the G string it is fret 4. Click a dot, then the open string above it, and you should hear the same note.

3Core

Intervals: measuring the distance

An interval is the distance between two notes. Play them together and you hear a harmonic interval; play them one after the other and you hear a melodic one. Every scale and chord is a recipe of intervals, so these thirteen names are the vocabulary for everything else.

Names have two parts. The number (2nd, 3rd, 5th) says how many letter names are spanned. The quality says the exact size. 4ths, 5ths and octaves are called perfect. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths come in two sizes, major (larger) and minor (one half step smaller).

The interval ladder

Bar length = number of half steps = number of frets on one string. ▶ plays the two notes apart, then together, starting from C.

Ear habit. Learn each interval as a sound as well as a number. Seconds and sevenths rub, thirds and sixths sound sweet, fourths, fifths and octaves sound open and hollow. Sing the top note after you play it.

5Core

Interval shapes, string by string

On one string an interval is simply its number of frets. Across strings it becomes a fixed shape you can move anywhere. Because neighbouring strings are five frets apart, moving to the next string "uses up" five half steps, so the shape is whatever is left over.

The catch is the B string. Whenever a shape crosses from the G string to the B string, the upper note moves one fret higher than it would elsewhere. Pick an interval below to see every practical shape, including the shifted ones.

Every interval at a glance

Upper note relative to the root's fret, for a root on the low E, A or D string staying below the B string.

"2 up" = two frets toward the body, "1 back" = one fret toward the nut, a dot = too far to reach. Add one fret to the upper note whenever the shape crosses from the G string to the B string.

Worked example: the octave. Root on the low E string: skip a string, two frets up (lands on the D string). Root on the A string: same shape, landing on the G string. Root on the D string: the upper note lands on the B string, so it is three frets up. Root on the G string: lands on the high E string, also three frets up, because that shape crosses the B string too.

Naming an interval properly

Find the number first, then the quality. The number comes only from the letters; sharps and flats do not change it.

1. Count lettersInclude both ends. C up to E touches C, D, E: three letters, so it is some kind of 3rd.

2. Count half stepsC to E is 4 half steps (4 frets on one string).

3. Match the qualityA 3rd of 4 half steps is major. A 3rd of 3 half steps is minor.

7Go deeper

Flipped and stretched intervals

Inversion. Take the lower note of an interval and move it up an octave, and you get its inversion. The two always add up to an octave: the numbers add to 9, the half steps add to 12, major swaps with minor, augmented swaps with diminished, and perfect stays perfect.

Each pair fills one octave. Knowing one of the pair gives you the other for free: a 6th shape is a 3rd shape turned upside down.

Compound intervals. Intervals wider than an octave keep the sound of their smaller version with more space in it. Subtract 7 from the number to find the simple interval. These are the numbers you see in chord names such as add9, 11 and 13.

8Checkpoint

Chapter 1 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Name the note at any fret on the low E and A strings.
  • Say which two pairs of natural notes are a half step apart, and which pair of strings is tuned differently.
  • Name all twelve intervals with their half-step counts, and recognise at least the 3rds, the 5th and the octave by ear.
  • Play a perfect 5th and an octave from a root on any string, including across the B string.
  • Spell an interval by letter name, such as a major 3rd above A♭ or a perfect 5th above B.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: pick a root, play each interval above it on one string, and say its name.
  2. Two minutes: play the same intervals as cross-string shapes from lesson 5, watching the B-string shift.
  3. Three minutes: name ten intervals with the method in lesson 6, then play each one.
  4. Three minutes: flip three intervals to their inversions and check that each pair adds up to 12.
