Two, three or four notes per string

How many notes you play on each string decides the shape of a scale. Three per string gives the even patterns of chapter 6. Two per string makes a compact shape that drifts toward the nut as it climbs. Four per string covers a huge range by sliding up the neck, useful for long runs that travel.

Arpeggios borrowed from other chords

Over any chord you can play the arpeggio of a different chord from the same scale. Because the two share notes, the borrowed arpeggio drops the root and adds upper notes such as the 9th, 11th or 13th. You already know the shapes; only the starting note changes.

5Go deeper

Triad pairs

Take two triads from the same scale that share no notes, and alternate between them. The six notes outline the harmony but sound more angular and modern than running the scale, because every group of three is a clear, strong shape.

6Go deeper

Four triads, twelve notes

A more experimental idea: some sets of four triads use all twelve notes with none repeated. Playing through such a set touches every note exactly once while each fragment still sounds like an ordinary chord, which gives lines that are free of any key but never random.

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Play one scale as a two-notes-per-string and a three-notes-per-string pattern.
  • Name the home chord of Lydian dominant, altered and Phrygian dominant.
  • Play a minor-major 7 and a diminished 7 arpeggio in one position.
  • Over a major 7 chord, play the minor 7 arpeggio from its 3rd and name the notes it adds.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Three minutes: one arpeggio type in two neighbouring positions.
  2. Three minutes: a borrowed arpeggio over a looped chord.
  3. Four minutes: one triad pair, alternating the triads up and down a position.
