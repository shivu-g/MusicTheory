1Core

Beat, bar and tempo

The beat is the steady pulse you tap your foot to. Beats are grouped into bars (also called measures), most often four at a time, with the first beat of each bar felt a little stronger. Tempo is how fast the beats go, measured in beats per minute.

Count "one, two, three, four" along with the clicks. The metronome in the top bar keeps this going for as long as you like.

2Core

Note lengths and subdivision

Every rhythm is made by dividing the beat into equal parts or by holding a note across several beats. Each division has a name and a way of counting it out loud. Counting aloud while you play is the single most effective rhythm exercise there is.

Each row is one bar of four beats. ▶ plays the note against the click.

3Core

Time signatures

The two numbers at the start of a piece say how the beats are grouped. The top number is how many beats are in a bar; the bottom number is which note length counts as one beat (4 means quarter note, 8 means eighth note).

4Core

Strumming patterns

Good strumming comes from one rule: the hand never stops. It swings down on every numbered beat and up on every "and", like a pendulum. A pattern is simply a choice of which swings touch the strings and which pass by in the air.

Pattern

Click any box to switch that strum on or off and build your own pattern. Filled boxes are played; empty ones are a silent swing of the hand. ↓ is a downstroke, ↑ an upstroke.

Practise it muted first. Lay your fretting hand lightly across the strings and strum the pattern as pure rhythm. Add the chord once your hand can keep the pendulum going on its own.
5Core

Syncopation

A rhythm that puts its weight on the beats sounds square and steady. Shift the accents onto the "ands", or let a chord arrive just before the beat, and the rhythm starts to push and pull against the pulse. That displacement is syncopation, and it is most of what makes funk, reggae, Latin and pop grooves feel alive.

Dark boxes are accented strums. Listen for where the weight falls compared with the click.

6Go deeper

Straight and swing

Eighth notes can be played evenly, splitting the beat exactly in half, or with a long-short lilt, where the first of each pair lasts about twice as long as the second. The second way is swing, the basic feel of blues, jazz and shuffle rhythms. It is usually not written differently; you are simply told to swing.

The same sixteen notes both times. Only the placement of every second note changes.

7Go deeper

Moving an idea in time

A short phrase sounds different depending on where in the bar it starts. Begin it on beat one and it is a statement. Begin it half a beat late and it becomes a syncopated answer. Moving a phrase like this, without changing a note, is one of the simplest ways to develop a solo.

One three-note idea, started in three different places.

8Checkpoint

Chapter 9 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Count quarter notes, eighth notes, triplets and sixteenth notes aloud against a click.
  • Tell 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8 apart by ear.
  • Strum a pattern with a missed downstroke while your hand keeps moving.
  • Play the same line straight and then swung.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: metronome at 70. Muted strums in quarters, then eighths, then sixteenths, counting aloud.
  2. Three minutes: one strumming pattern over a two-chord change, without stopping at the change.
  3. Three minutes: the off-beat pattern from lesson 5, slowly, then faster.
  4. Two minutes: a scale in swung eighth notes.
