1Core

Pentatonic and blues scales

Remove the two notes of the major scale that sit a half step from a neighbour (the 4th and 7th) and five notes remain: the major pentatonic. With no half steps there is nothing to clash, which is why it sounds good over almost anything in the key. Start the same five notes from the 6th and you get the minor pentatonic, the most-used scale in rock and blues guitar. Add one extra note, the ♭5, and it becomes the blues scale.

Root
Scale

Two for one. A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic are the same five notes. Learn one set of shapes and you have both: treat the root three frets higher as home and the minor shapes become major.
2Core

The five pentatonic boxes

The pentatonic scale has only two notes on each string, so its five positions are simple box shapes. Box 1 of the minor pentatonic, with the root under your first finger on the low E string, is the most played shape in rock and blues. The other four connect it to the rest of the neck.

Root
Box

Connect two boxes first. Slide from the top of box 1 into box 2 on the G and B strings and back. Two connected boxes are far more useful than five separate ones.
3Core

Five positions of one scale

A scale covers the whole neck, which is too much to take in at once. The usual answer is to cut it into five overlapping positions, each about four frets wide and each wrapped around one of the five chord shapes from chapter 4. Learn the five, and you can play in any key without moving your hand far.

Key
Position

The green band is the position. Bright dots are one workable fingering inside it; faded dots are the rest of the scale. Numbers are scale degrees and R is the root.

Natural minor is the same five shapes. A minor has the same notes as C major, so its positions are C major's positions with a different note treated as home. Switch between Major and Natural minor above and watch only the labels change.

Three notes per string

A second way to lay out a seven-note scale is to play exactly three notes on every string. The shapes are wider, with more stretching, but the picking is perfectly even, which makes them popular for fast runs. There are seven patterns, one starting from each note of the scale.

5Core

Arpeggios: chords played one note at a time

An arpeggio is a chord with its notes played in sequence. On guitar it is the bridge between chords and melody: the arpeggio shows you which notes of the scale position belong to the chord of the moment. Each arpeggio shape sits inside one of the five positions.

6Core

Practice patterns

Running a scale straight up and down teaches your fingers the shape but not much music. Sequences break the scale into small repeating cells. They build control and they are the raw material of real melodic lines.

Key
Pattern

Apply every pattern three ways. Up, down, and in a second position. Then try the same pattern on a pentatonic box.
7Core

Soloing over the blues

The 12-bar blues uses three dominant 7 chords, and there are three common ways to play over it. Most players mix all three.

  • One scale throughout: the minor pentatonic or blues scale of the key. Simple, and its clash with the major chords is the blues sound.
  • Sweeten it: switch to the major pentatonic of the key over the I chord.
  • Follow the chords: aim for each chord's 3rd and ♭7 as it arrives.
Key
Chord

Minor pentatonic box 1. Small grey dots are the pentatonic; coloured dots are the tones of the current chord, labelled from its root. Some chord tones lie outside the box: those are the notes that make a blues solo follow the changes.

8Go deeper

Walking bass lines

A walking bass plays one note on every beat and connects each chord to the next. A reliable recipe for a bar with one chord: root, a step up, another chord tone, then a note a half step away from the next root. On guitar, playing these on the bottom strings is the first step toward accompanying yourself with bass and chords together.

Key
Form

Dark chips are roots; red chips are half-step approach notes leading to the next root.

9Checkpoint

Chapter 6 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Play minor pentatonic box 1 in any key, and connect it to box 2.
  • Play a major scale in at least two neighbouring positions and move between them.
  • Play a major 7, minor 7 and dominant 7 arpeggio inside one scale position.
  • Play a scale in thirds and in groups of three without stopping.
  • Over a blues, land on the 3rd of the I chord and the 3rd of the IV chord as each arrives.
  • Walk a bass line through a 12-bar blues.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: one major scale position, slowly, saying the degree numbers.
  2. Three minutes: the same position in thirds, then in groups of three.
  3. Two minutes: pentatonic boxes 1 and 2, sliding between them.
  4. Three minutes: improvise over a blues using only box 1 plus each chord's 3rd.
