1Core

Chord tones are the targets

A scale tells you which notes are available. It does not tell you which ones sound right at a given moment. The notes that sound settled are the notes of the chord being played underneath. A melody that lands on chord tones on the strong beats will sound as if it belongs, even with no accompaniment.

In a ii – V – I all three chords come from one scale, so the scale never changes. What changes is which four notes of it are the targets.

2Core

Guide tones: the 3rd and 7th

Of a chord's four notes, the 3rd and 7th say the most about it. Follow them through a progression and you get two smooth lines that move by a half step or not at all. Those lines are called guide tones, and a solo built around them outlines the harmony clearly.

Key

ii – V – I. Each row is one line you could sing or play; together they are enough to hear the chords.

3Go deeper

Approach notes and enclosures

Notes outside the scale are not wrong if they are on their way somewhere. A note a half step below a chord tone, played just before it, makes the arrival stronger. Circle the target from both sides and you have an enclosure, one of the signature sounds of jazz lines.

Chord

Dark chips are the chord tones being targeted. Light chips are the approach notes.

5Core

Soloing over the blues

The 12-bar blues uses three dominant 7 chords, and there are three common ways to play over it. Most players mix all three.

  • One scale throughout: the minor pentatonic or blues scale of the key. Simple, and its clash with the major chords is the blues sound.
  • Sweeten it: switch to the major pentatonic of the key over the I chord.
  • Follow the chords: aim for each chord's 3rd and ♭7 as it arrives.

6Go deeper

Pentatonics in new places

You already know the pentatonic shapes. Play them from a note other than the chord's root and the same five-note shape picks out a different, more colourful set of notes against the chord. It is the quickest way to sound more sophisticated without learning a new fingering.

7Core

Phrasing: saying something

Right notes are not yet music. What makes a solo memorable is how the notes are grouped. Four habits do most of the work.

MotifStart with a short idea of three to five notes that you could sing back.
RepeatPlay it again. Repetition is what tells the listener it was an idea.
VaryChange one thing: the last note, the rhythm, or the octave.
Leave spaceStop. A rest lets the phrase land and gives you time to hear the next one.
Key

A four-bar example in the minor pentatonic. Play each step, then all four in a row.

Sing first. If you cannot sing a phrase, you are probably playing a finger pattern. Sing a short idea, then find it on the guitar.
8Core

How to practise

Short, regular and slow beats long, occasional and fast. Practise at a speed where you make no mistakes, then raise it a little at a time. A metronome keeps you honest.

Metronome

The same metronome sits in the bar at the top of every page. The first beat of every four is higher. Start around 60 to 80 and only move up when a pattern is clean three times in a row.

9Checkpoint

Chapter 7 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Name the chord tones of each chord in a ii – V – I and find them in one position.
  • Play the two guide-tone lines through a ii – V – I in two keys.
  • Approach any chord tone from a half step below.
  • Over a blues, land on the 3rd of the I chord and the 3rd of the IV chord as each arrives.
  • Play a four-bar phrase built from one motif, with at least one bar of space.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: guide tones only, through ii – V – I, with the metronome.
  2. Three minutes: add one approach note before each guide tone.
  3. Three minutes: improvise over a blues using only box 1 plus each chord's 3rd.
  4. Two minutes: one motif, repeated and varied, with rests.
