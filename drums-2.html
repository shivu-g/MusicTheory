Time signatures

How beats are grouped into bars, and why 6/8 feels different from 3/4 even though both fill a bar with six eighths.

Beats per bar, and how each beat divides

A time signature answers two questions. The top number says how many counted units sit in a bar. The bottom number says which note value is the unit: 4 is a quarter note, 8 is an eighth note. Two things then decide how a meter feels: how many beats you feel in the bar, and how each beat divides.

  • Duple meters have two beats (2/4, 2/2, 6/8). Triple meters have three (3/4, 3/2, 9/8). Quadruple meters have four (4/4, 4/2, 12/8).
  • Simple meters (2/4, 3/4, 4/4) divide each beat in two. Compound meters (6/8, 9/8, 12/8) divide each beat in three.

So 6/8 is not six equal beats. It is two big beats of three eighths each, counted 1 la li 2 la li. It holds the same six eighths as 3/4, but 3/4 groups them in twos (three beats) and 6/8 groups them in threes (two beats). That is why the two sound so different.

Odd meters are grouped pulses

Meters like 5/4 and 7/8 are usually felt as short groups of two and three added together. 7/8 can be 2+2+3, 3+2+2 or 2+3+2: the same seven eighths, three different accent patterns. Count each group from 1 and the groups become easy to lock to.

Meter and accent player

Meter
Grouping

One click per written unit (a quarter in x/4, an eighth in x/8), so the bpm shown is clicks per minute. The high click is beat 1 of the bar, the middle click starts each other group.

On the kit. Keep the hi-hat steady on every unit and put the bass drum on the start of each group. When the groups change, the whole feel changes while the hi-hat does not.

Practise this

  • Play 4/4, 3/4 and 6/8 with the same hi-hat speed and notice where the bass drum lands.
  • Take 7/8 in each of its three groupings and say the counts aloud before you add the kit.
  • Count 6/8 as two beats ("1 la li 2 la li") and then as six, and feel how the second way flattens it.
