1Core

Intervals by ear

Theory becomes useful when you can hear it. The first step is recognising intervals, because melodies are chains of intervals and chords are stacks of them. Start with a few very different ones and add more as they become easy.

Sing it back. Before you answer, hum both notes. If you can sing an interval you can recognise it, and the other way round.
2Core

Chord qualities by ear

Next, learn the colour of each chord type. Do not try to pick out individual notes. Listen to the overall mood: bright, dark, tense or unsettled.

3Go deeper

Progressions by ear

The same handful of progressions appear in thousands of songs. Once you know their sound in numerals, you can often name a song's chords on first hearing, in whatever key it happens to be in.

Follow the bass. The lowest note usually plays the root of each chord. Hum the bass line and you have the progression's shape.

Capo and transposing

A capo clamps across a fret and raises every open string by that many half steps. It lets you keep easy open-chord shapes while the music sounds in a different key, which is useful for matching a singer's range. Thinking in numerals makes the conversion mechanical: the shapes are the numerals of one key, the sound is the numerals of another.

I want to sound in

Each row is one way to get that key with open-chord shapes. "Shapes" are what your hands play; the sounding chords are what the listener hears.

7Checkpoint

Chapter 7 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Score eight out of ten on the easy interval and triad trainers.
  • Recognise I – IV – V – I and I – V – vi – IV by ear.
  • Find the key of a song from its chord list.
  • Move a song to a new key, with and without a capo.
  • Take one song through all six steps of the method above.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Three minutes: interval trainer, one level harder than is comfortable.
  2. Two minutes: chord trainer.
  3. Five minutes: put on a song you like and find its key and first two chords by ear.
