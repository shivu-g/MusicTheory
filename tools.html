The Scale Harmonizer

Choose a scale and a key. The row of cards shows the chord built on each degree using only notes from that scale, with its Roman numeral and the mode that starts on that degree. Click a card to hear the chord. The fretboard and piano show the scale itself.

Scale
Chords
Key
Major type Minor type Dominant 7 Diminished or augmented

W = whole step (2 frets), H = half step (1 fret), W+H = 3 frets. Harmonic major and double harmonic are rarer scales included for completeness; a chord marked * is an altered stack outside the basic chord types. A ♭ before a numeral means that degree is a half step lower than in the major scale.

What to notice

  • Major scale: only one dominant 7 chord, on V. That is why V7 points so clearly at I.
  • Natural minor: the same seven chords as its relative major, renumbered from a new home. The dominant 7 moves to ♭VII.
  • Harmonic minor: V becomes a dominant 7 and vii becomes a fully diminished 7. Both lead strongly back to i. The i chord gains a major 7th on top of a minor triad.
  • Melodic minor: two dominant 7 chords side by side on IV and V, and two half-diminished chords on vi and vii. Its modes supply many of the scales jazz players use over altered dominants.
Practice loop. Set the harmonizer to seventh chords, pick one key, and play i, iv and V from each of the three minor scales with the shell voicings from chapter 8. Listen for how the V chord changes the moment the 7th of the scale is raised.

The Drop Voicing Builder

Chapter 8 introduced drop 2 and drop 3. They belong to a family. Start with a seventh chord stacked in thirds (close position), number its notes from the top, and drop one or two of them an octave. Each recipe spreads the chord differently, and each one gives four inversions.

3Tool

Open-String Chord Finder

Letting open strings ring against fretted notes higher up the neck gives a shimmering sound that no barre chord can produce. There is no fixed system for these, so this finder searches for them: it looks for shapes with the root in the bass where every open string used is a note of the chord.

Root
Chord

Small dots to the left of the nut are open strings. Keys with sharps that match the open strings (E, A, D, G) give the most results.

2, 3 and 4 Notes-Per-String Explorer

How many notes you play on each string decides the shape of a scale. Three per string gives the even patterns of chapter 6. Two per string makes a compact shape that drifts toward the nut as it climbs. Four per string covers a huge range by sliding up the neck, useful for long runs that travel.

Twelve-Tone Triad Combinatorics

A more experimental idea: some sets of four triads use all twelve notes with none repeated. Playing through such a set touches every note exactly once while each fragment still sounds like an ordinary chord, which gives lines that are free of any key but never random.
