Triads and inversions

Turn any triad into three shapes by moving the bottom note to the top, and see which right-hand fingers fit each shape.

One chord, three shapes

A triad has three notes: the root, the 3rd and the 5th. In root position the root is the lowest note. Take that bottom note and move it up an octave and you get first inversion, with the 3rd now at the bottom. Do it again and you get second inversion, with the 5th at the bottom. It is still the same chord; only the order changes.

Inversions let your hand stay in one place while the chords change, instead of jumping around the keyboard. That is the whole point of learning them.

Right-hand fingering

In root position the right hand uses 1-3-5: thumb, middle finger, little finger. In first inversion the gaps change. The bottom two notes are a third apart and the top two a fourth apart, so the index finger fits comfortably in the middle: 1-2-5. Second inversion goes back to 1-3-5.

For the left hand, root position is normally 5-3-1, little finger on the root. Left-hand fingerings for the inversions are not listed here yet. Treat every fingering as a convention that suits the hand, not a rule. For example, using 1-2-5 on a root-position triad would add an awkward stretch.

Name check. The inversion number tells you which chord tone is at the bottom: first inversion has the 3rd, second inversion has the 5th. Written as a slash chord it is C/E and C/G, with the bass note after the slash.
