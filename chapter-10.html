1Core

The seven modes, bright to dark

Start a major scale from each of its seven notes and you get seven modes. Comparing them all from the same root is more useful: arranged this way, each mode is the one above it with a single note lowered by a half step.

Highlighted degrees differ from the plain major scale (Ionian). "Home" is the degree of a major key where that mode naturally sits. ▶ plays each mode from C so you can compare them.

One note gives each mode its flavour. Lydian is major with a ♯4. Mixolydian is major with a ♭7. Dorian is natural minor with a raised 6th. Phrygian is natural minor with a ♭2. Lean on that one note and the mode becomes audible.
2Core

Matching a scale to a chord

A scale fits a chord when it contains the chord's tones. The chord tones are your safe landing notes; the other scale notes add colour between them. Pick a root and a mode to see both layers.

Mode

Root Chord tones (3rd, 5th, 7th) Other scale notes
  • Major 7 chords: Ionian when the chord is I, Lydian when it is IV or when you want a floating sound.
  • Minor 7 chords: Dorian on ii, Phrygian on iii, Aeolian on vi. Dorian is the usual first choice for a minor 7 on its own.
  • Dominant 7 chords: Mixolydian.
  • Half-diminished chords: Locrian.
Practice loop. Record or loop one chord. Play only the coloured chord tones for a minute, then add the grey notes as passing notes between them, always landing on a coloured dot at the end of a phrase.
3Core

Modes as home keys

Lessons 1 and 2 treated modes as scales to play over a chord. A mode can also be a key: a piece can live in D Dorian the way it can live in C major. Two things make that work. The mode's first chord has to feel like home, and the progression has to feature the chord containing the mode's characteristic note, the one note that separates it from plain major or minor.

Mode
Root

Avoid the pull of the parent key. D Dorian uses the notes of C major, and a G7 chord would drag the ear straight to C. Modal pieces keep to two or three chords and keep returning to the home chord for that reason.

Brighter and darker on one root

A piece can change mode without changing its home note, for example from C Ionian to C Mixolydian. Because each mode in the bright-to-dark order differs from its neighbour by one note, moving one step along the order changes one note and shifts the mood a shade. Moving several steps at once is a bigger jolt.

Root
Bright → dark

Mirror pairs

A curiosity that explains the order: write a mode's pattern of whole and half steps backwards and you get another mode. The pairs sit opposite each other in the bright-to-dark list.

6Go deeper

Voicings built in fourths

Chords do not have to be built in thirds. Stack notes of a scale a 4th apart and you get open, modern-sounding voicings that do not state major or minor as plainly. They are called quartal voicings, and moving one shape up through a scale gives seven of them.

Dorian on

Three-note stacks through the Dorian mode. Most are two perfect 4ths (a straight line across the strings); the ones marked in red contain a tritone.

How to use them. Over a single minor 7 chord, any of the seven shapes from that chord's Dorian mode will work. Slide between two neighbours for movement without changing the harmony.

Chapter 10 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Name the one note that gives Dorian, Lydian and Mixolydian their flavour.
  • Choose a workable mode for a major 7, a minor 7 and a dominant 7 chord.
  • Play a two-chord vamp in Dorian and in Mixolydian and name the characteristic note of each.
  • Explain why a modal progression avoids the V7 of its parent key.
  • Play a voicing built in fourths on one set of strings.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Three minutes: loop a Dorian vamp and improvise, leaning on the natural 6th.
  2. Three minutes: the same with a Mixolydian vamp and its ♭7.
  3. Two minutes: play the chords of one mode and say which one carries the characteristic note.
  4. Two minutes: build one quartal voicing on a chord of a Dorian vamp.
