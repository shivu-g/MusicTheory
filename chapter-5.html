1Core

Five shapes cover the whole neck

The five open major chords every guitarist learns first are C, A, G, E and D. Each one is a movable shape: slide it up the neck, let your index finger do the job the nut was doing, and it becomes a different chord. Placed in the order C, A, G, E, D, the five shapes link end to end, so any chord can be played in five positions. This is often called the CAGED system.

Why it matters. The shapes are also landmarks. Each one has a scale position and an arpeggio wrapped around it, which is how chapter 6 organises the neck.
2Core

Barre chords from the E and A shapes

Two of the five shapes do most of the everyday work. The E shape has its root on the low E string and the A shape has its root on the A string. Know the note names on those two strings and you can play any major, minor or seventh chord anywhere.

Root finder: the two bottom strings

The dark dots are your chosen root. The root sits under your index finger in both shapes.

Power chords. Play only the lowest two or three notes of either shape (root, 5th and the octave) and you have a power chord. With no 3rd in it, it is neither major nor minor, which is why it works over both.
3Core

Slash chords and inversions

A slash chord names a chord and then the bass note to play under it: C/E is a C chord with E as the lowest note. When the bass note belongs to the chord it is an inversion. When it does not, the bass adds a new colour to the chord.

Spread triads and pedal points

Two more things to do with three notes and a bass. A spread triad takes the middle note of a triad and moves it up an octave, so the chord covers more than an octave. It sounds open and piano-like. A pedal point does the opposite job: the bass holds one note while the chords above it change, building tension until the bass finally moves.

5Go deeper

Drop 2 and drop 3 voicings

Seventh chords stacked in plain thirds are often unplayable on guitar. The fix is to take the second note from the top and drop it an octave. The result, a drop 2 voicing, fits neatly on four neighbouring strings. There are three such string sets: the top four, the middle four and the bottom four. Each chord has four of them, one for each chord tone in the bass, and together they walk up the neck.

Drop 3 works the same way but drops the third note from the top. The bass note ends up one string further away, with a string skipped in between, which gives a wider, fuller sound suited to playing alone or with a singer.

A scale of chords up the neck

Take one kind of voicing and move it through all seven chords of a key, and you have a chord scale: the harmonised scale from chapter 2, laid out along one set of strings. It is the fastest way to learn where every chord of a key sits, and it gives you a chord for any melody note.

7Go deeper

Voicings built in fourths

Chords do not have to be built in thirds. Stack notes of a scale a 4th apart and you get open, modern-sounding voicings that do not state major or minor as plainly. They are called quartal voicings, and moving one shape up through a scale gives seven of them.

Dorian on

Three-note stacks through the Dorian mode. Most are two perfect 4ths (a straight line across the strings); the ones marked in red contain a tritone.

How to use them. Over a single minor 7 chord, any of the seven shapes from that chord's Dorian mode will work. Slide between two neighbours for movement without changing the harmony.
8Core

Voice leading: the shortest path

Good chord playing is less about the shapes and more about how you get from one to the next. Treat each note in a chord as a singer: if a note is shared with the next chord, keep it; otherwise move it to the nearest note of the next chord. In a ii – V – I, the 3rd and 7th of each chord do this almost by themselves.

Key

Shell voicings from chapter 2. Each voice either stays where it is or falls by one step.

9Checkpoint

Chapter 5 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Play one major chord in all five shapes, in order up the neck.
  • Find any major, minor or dominant 7 chord as an E-shape and an A-shape barre.
  • Explain what C/E and C/G are, and play a descending bass line under a chord progression.
  • Play a ii – V – I with shell voicings where the upper notes move by a step or not at all.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Three minutes: one chord, five shapes, up and back. Change the chord each day.
  2. Three minutes: I – IV – V in one key using only E-shape and A-shape barres, staying within five frets.
  3. Two minutes: the walking bass line from lesson 3 in two keys.
  4. Two minutes: ii – V – I shells in three keys, watching the 3rds and 7ths.
