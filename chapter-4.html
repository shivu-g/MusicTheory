Swapping chords that do the same job

Stack the notes of a key in thirds and the seven seventh chords appear as a staircase: each chord is the one above it shifted along by one note. Neighbours on the staircase share three of their four notes, which is why they can replace each other with so little disturbance.

Key
Tonic Subdominant Dominant

Columns are the notes of the key arranged in thirds. R, 3, 5 and 7 show each note's role in that chord.

  • For I: try vi or iii. The melody note usually decides which one works.
  • For IV: try ii. In jazz the ii chord is the default and IV is the alternative.
  • For V: try vii°. It is a V7 chord with the root removed.
2Core

Secondary dominants

The V7 chord pulls strongly toward I. You can borrow that pull for any other major or minor chord in the key by placing its own V7 in front of it, as if that chord were briefly the tonic. Each secondary dominant brings in at least one note from outside the key, and that note is the sound of the device.

Key

Read "V7/ii" as "five-seven of two": the dominant 7th chord built a 5th above the ii chord.

On the neck. The dominant's root is a 5th above its target, which is the same fret on the next thicker string. If your target chord has its root on the A string, its secondary dominant has its root at the same fret on the low E string.
Go one step further. Any dominant 7 can be turned into a ii–V by putting the minor 7 chord a 5th above it in front. One bar of V7 becomes half a bar of ii7 and half a bar of V7.

Tritone substitution

The tension in a dominant 7 chord comes from its 3rd and 7th, which sit a tritone apart. The dominant 7 chord whose root is a tritone away contains the same two notes with their roles swapped. So one can replace the other, and the bass line turns from a leap into a half-step slide.

Key
Original
With the substitute

On the neck. In a shell voicing the 3rd and 7th stay on exactly the same two frets. Only the bass note moves, and it lands one fret above the root of the I chord you are heading for.

Borrowing from the parallel minor

A major key can borrow chords from the minor key that shares its home note. This is called modal mixture. The borrowed chords bring in the lowered 3rd, 6th and 7th, which darken the colour without leaving the key centre.

    Diminished passing chords

    When two chords in a key have roots a whole step apart, a diminished 7 chord fits on the fret between them. It works because it is really the secondary dominant of the chord you are heading to, with its root left out, so the bass climbs by half steps.

    Key

    A diminished 7 chord repeats every three frets, so any of its four notes can be treated as the root.

    Reharmonising cheat sheet

    The six moves from this chapter in one place, spelled out in the key you have selected. Apply one at a time to a progression you already know and listen to what changes.

    Key
    Practice loop. Take I – vi – ii – V in one key. Play it plain, then swap one chord per pass using a different move each time. Keep the top note of your voicings as still as you can, so you hear the harmony change underneath it.

    A ten-minute routine

    1. Two minutes: play I – vi – ii – V plain, in one key.
    2. Three minutes: add a secondary dominant before the ii, then before the vi.
    3. Three minutes: swap the V for its tritone substitute, then try iv in place of ii.
    4. Two minutes: play your favourite version from memory in a second key.
