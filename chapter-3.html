1Core

Chord function: home, away, tension

The seven chords of a key do three jobs. Some feel like home, some move away from it, and some create tension that wants to fall back home. Chords that do the same job can often stand in for one another, which is the basis of chord substitution.

  • Tonic (I, iii, vi): rest. iii and vi each share two of the three notes of the I chord, so they sound like shades of home.
  • Subdominant (ii, IV): motion. They contain the 4th note of the key, which pulls gently away from home.
  • Dominant (V, vii°): tension. They contain the 7th note, a half step below home, and in their seventh-chord form a tritone that wants to resolve.

Most progressions travel around this triangle in the direction of the arrows, although skipping a corner or doubling back is common.

Cadences: how phrases end

A cadence is the pair of chords at the end of a phrase. It works like punctuation: some endings sound finished, some sound like a pause, and one deliberately misleads the ear.

V → IAuthenticG7 → CA full stop. The strongest way to end.
IV → IPlagalF → CThe "amen" ending. Gentle and settled.
… → VHalfC → F → GA comma. The phrase stops on tension and needs an answer.
V → viDeceptiveG7 → AmA surprise. You expect I and get its darker stand-in.

The seven modes, bright to dark

Start a major scale from each of its seven notes and you get seven modes. Comparing them all from the same root is more useful: arranged this way, each mode is the one above it with a single note lowered by a half step.

Highlighted degrees differ from the plain major scale (Ionian). "Home" is the degree of a major key where that mode naturally sits. ▶ plays each mode from C so you can compare them.

One note gives each mode its flavour. Lydian is major with a ♯4. Mixolydian is major with a ♭7. Dorian is natural minor with a raised 6th. Phrygian is natural minor with a ♭2. Lean on that one note and the mode becomes audible.

Matching a scale to a chord

A scale fits a chord when it contains the chord's tones. The chord tones are your safe landing notes; the other scale notes add colour between them. Pick a root and a mode to see both layers.

Root
Mode

Root Chord tones (3rd, 5th, 7th) Other scale notes
  • Major 7 chords: Ionian when the chord is I, Lydian when it is IV or when you want a floating sound.
  • Minor 7 chords: Dorian on ii, Phrygian on iii, Aeolian on vi. Dorian is the usual first choice for a minor 7 on its own.
  • Dominant 7 chords: Mixolydian.
  • Half-diminished chords: Locrian.
Practice loop. Record or loop one chord. Play only the coloured chord tones for a minute, then add the grey notes as passing notes between them, always landing on a coloured dot at the end of a phrase.
6Core

Pentatonic and blues scales

Remove the two notes of the major scale that sit a half step from a neighbour (the 4th and 7th) and five notes remain: the major pentatonic. With no half steps there is nothing to clash, which is why it sounds good over almost anything in the key. Start the same five notes from the 6th and you get the minor pentatonic, the most-used scale in rock and blues guitar. Add one extra note, the ♭5, and it becomes the blues scale.

Root
Scale

Two for one. A minor pentatonic and C major pentatonic are the same five notes. Learn one set of shapes and you have both: treat the root three frets higher as home and the minor shapes become major.

Chapter 3 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Say whether each of the seven chords in a key is tonic, subdominant or dominant.
  • Write a progression in Roman numerals and play it in a second key.
  • Tell the four cadences apart by ear.
  • Play the minor pentatonic in one position and find the major pentatonic that shares its notes.
  • Name the one note that gives Dorian, Lydian and Mixolydian their flavour.
  • Choose a workable mode for a major 7, a minor 7 and a dominant 7 chord.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: play I – IV – V – I in two keys, saying each chord's job aloud.
  2. Three minutes: play I – V – vi – IV, then its minor twin i – ♭VI – ♭III – ♭VII.
  3. Three minutes: loop one minor 7 chord and improvise with the minor pentatonic, landing on chord tones.
  4. Two minutes: over the same chord, add the 2nd and 6th to turn the pentatonic into Dorian.
