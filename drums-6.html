Syncopation and polyrhythm

Put accents where the ear does not expect them, then play two different divisions of time at once.

Strong beats, weak beats, off-beats

In 4/4, beat 1 is the strongest, beat 3 comes next, and beats 2 and 4 are weaker. The "&" between beats is an off-beat. Syncopation is accenting a weak position at the expense of a strong one while the pulse keeps running underneath. Note the difference in words: "off-beat" names a place, "syncopation" is the effect it creates.

Four common devices:

  • Off-beat accents: notes on the "&"s.
  • Missed downbeat: a rest where a strong beat would be.
  • Anticipation: a note placed an eighth or sixteenth early, often held through the beat.
  • Tie across the beat: a note started before a beat and held over it.

A common recipe is a straight foundation (such as four-on-the-floor kick) with a syncopated layer on top, for example snare on 1, 2, 3 and the "and" of 3. To write your own: write the rhythm straight, move one or two notes, remove a note on a strong beat, keep one voice straight as an anchor, loop it, and check you can still feel the pulse.

Tresillo and the son clave

Tresillo is the rhythm 3+3+2: over eight sixteenth steps, hits on steps 0, 3 and 6. It is a very common syncopated cell, felt as a group of three pulling against the group-of-two meter. It turns up in the Bo Diddley beat, reggaeton and dembow patterns, and second-line drumming. Played twice in a bar it gives hits on steps 0, 3, 6, 8, 11, 14.

The 3-2 son clave is a two-bar pattern with three hits in the first bar and two in the second. Folded into one 16-step bar the hits land on steps 0, 3, 6, 10 and 12 (gaps of 3, 3, 4, 2, 4). The 2-3 clave is the same pattern turned half a cycle, so it starts with the two-hit half: steps 2, 4, 8, 11, 14. Which side comes first is a chart convention; the rhythm is the same one. The three-side crosses the beat and builds tension, the two-side lands and releases it. Try both presets above.

Polyrhythm

A polyrhythm sounds two different divisions of the same time span together. 3:2 means three evenly spaced notes in the time of two (musicians also call this a hemiola). Count a shared timeline of 6 steps: one hand plays steps 0, 2, 4 and the other plays 0 and 3. The attacks in order are: both hands, the three-hand, the two-hand, the three-hand. Speak one syllable per attack and you get the well-known phrase "nice cup of tea".

The same arithmetic works for any ratio a:b. The shared timeline is a×b steps long when the numbers share no factor, one hand plays every b-th step and the other every a-th. So 4:3 uses 12 steps (hands on 0, 3, 6, 9 and on 0, 4, 8), and 5:4 uses 20 steps. Those step lists come from the ratio itself. Learn each by ear first at a slow tempo such as 60 bpm and add the counting afterwards.

Practise this

  • Play the straight kick with one hand or foot and tap the "and" of each beat with the other, counting 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & aloud.
  • Take any preset, move a single note by one step, and ask whether the pulse still feels clear.
  • Say "nice cup of tea" while tapping 3:2 on your knees, then swap which hand takes the three.
