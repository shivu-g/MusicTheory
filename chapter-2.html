1Core

Seventh chords: stacking three thirds

A seventh chord is a triad with one more third on top: root, 3rd, 5th and 7th. The triad underneath sets the basic colour and the 7th adds a second layer of tension or softness. Seven combinations cover almost everything you will meet.

Read each stack from the bottom up. Examples are built on C.

Two notes do the talking. The 3rd and the 7th tell the ear which chord it is. The root is usually covered by the bass and the 5th is the same in most types, so guitarists often leave it out.

Shell voicings: root, 3rd and 7th only

Three notes, movable anywhere, and enough to state the chord clearly. Top row has the root on the low E string, bottom row on the A string. Play only the dotted strings and mute the rest. Nut is to the left. Click a diagram to hear it.

One fret at a time. Start on the maj7 shell. Lower the 7th one fret and it becomes a dominant 7. Lower the 3rd one fret as well and it becomes a minor 7. Hearing that sequence is the fastest way to learn the three main chord families.

More chord colours: sus, 6, add9 and 9

Not every chord is a plain stack of thirds. A few very common ones swap or add a single note. They are also in the chord list of the neck explorer above.

All built on C. ▶ plays the notes one by one, then together.

  • Suspended (sus): the 3rd is replaced by the 2nd or the 4th, so the chord is neither major nor minor. It sounds open and usually resolves back to the 3rd.
  • 6 chords: a triad with the 6th added. A softer alternative to a major 7 or minor 7.
  • add9: a triad plus the 9th (the 2nd an octave up) and no 7th.
  • 9 chords: a seventh chord with the 9th on top. The number tells you the highest note; the 7th is assumed to be underneath.
4Core

Reading chord symbols

A chord symbol is read left to right. Each part answers one question, and anything not written takes its default.

PartWhat it tells youExamples
1RootThe note the chord is built on. Always a capital letter, with ♯ or ♭ if needed.C, F♯, B♭
2Triad qualityNothing written means major. m or − means minor, ° or dim means diminished, + or aug means augmented.C, Cm, C°, C+
3SeventhA bare 7 always means the lowered 7th (♭7). The natural 7th has to be spelled out as maj7.C7, Cmaj7, Cm7
4Extension9, 11 or 13 in place of the 7 means the 7th is included and that note is stacked on top.C9, Cm11, C13
5AlterationA ♯ or ♭ in front of a number raises or lowers that one chord tone.Cm7♭5, C7♯9, C7♭13
6sus / addsus replaces the 3rd with the 2nd or 4th. add puts in one extra note without a 7th.Csus4, Cadd9
7SlashThe letter after the slash is the lowest note to play. Used for inversions and moving bass lines.C/E, C/G, D/C
The classic trap. C7 and Cmaj7 differ by one fret on the 7th but do opposite jobs: Cmaj7 is a chord of rest, C7 is a chord of tension. Likewise "Cm7" is minor triad plus ♭7, while "Cm(maj7)" is minor triad plus natural 7.
5Core

Relative and parallel minor

Every major key has two different minor partners, and they are easy to confuse.

C major
A minor relative
C minor parallel

Dark chip = home note. Purple chips = notes lowered by a half step compared with C major.

  • Relative minor uses exactly the same seven notes and the same key signature, but treats the 6th note as home. C major and A minor are relatives.
  • Parallel minor keeps the same home note and lowers the 3rd, 6th and 7th. C major and C minor are parallel.
On the neck. The relative minor root is three frets below the major root on the same string: C at fret 8 of the low E string, A at fret 5. Going the other way, the relative major is three frets above the minor root.
6Core

Why there are three minor scales

Natural minor has one weakness: its 7th note sits a whole step below home, so the chord built on the 5th degree is a minor chord with little pull. Composers fixed that by raising notes, and each fix became a scale of its own.

Natural minor 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 ♭7
Soft, modal sound. The v chord is minor, so the return home is gentle.
→
Harmonic minor 1 2 ♭3 4 5 ♭6 7
Raise the 7th. Now the V chord is a dominant 7 and pulls hard to i. Side effect: a three-fret jump from ♭6 to 7.
→
Melodic minor 1 2 ♭3 4 5 6 7
Raise the 6th too. The jump disappears and lines run smoothly. It is a major scale with a lowered 3rd.

Underlined degrees are the raised notes. ▶ plays each scale from C. Real minor-key music mixes all three freely, often within one phrase.

W = whole step (2 frets), H = half step (1 fret), W+H = 3 frets. A ♭ before a numeral means that degree is a half step lower than in the major scale.

What to notice

  • Major scale: only one dominant 7 chord, on V. That is why V7 points so clearly at I.
  • Natural minor: the same seven chords as its relative major, renumbered from a new home. The dominant 7 moves to ♭VII.
  • Harmonic minor: V becomes a dominant 7 and vii becomes a fully diminished 7. Both lead strongly back to i. The i chord gains a major 7th on top of a minor triad.
  • Melodic minor: two dominant 7 chords side by side on IV and V, and two half-diminished chords on vi and vii. Its modes supply many of the scales jazz players use over altered dominants.
Practice loop. Set the harmonizer to seventh chords, pick one key, and play i, iv and V from each of the three minor scales with the shell voicings from lesson 2. Listen for how the V chord changes the moment the 7th of the scale is raised.

The practical minor key

Because the three minor scales are mixed freely, a minor key in real music has more than seven chords to choose from. This is the palette you will actually meet, with the scale each chord comes from.

Key

Click ▶ on a card to hear the chord. Green cards are the minor ii – V – i, the minor-key version of the most common cadence in jazz.

  • V7 instead of v: the raised 7th from harmonic minor is used on the V chord almost every time the music wants to return home.
  • ♭III, ♭VI and ♭VII: the three major chords from natural minor. They give minor-key rock and pop its sound.
  • iiø7 – V7 – i: the half-diminished chord on ii leads to V7, which leads home.
9Checkpoint

Chapter 2 checkpoint

You are ready to move on when you can

  • Build a major 7, dominant 7 and minor 7 chord from any root, and hear the difference between them.
  • Play the three shell voicings with the root on the low E string and on the A string.
  • Read a symbol such as Cm7♭5, Gsus4 or D/F♯ and say which notes it asks for.
  • Find the relative minor of any major key, and explain how it differs from the parallel minor.
  • Say which note harmonic minor raises, which two melodic minor raises, and why.
  • Name the three chords of a ii – V – i in a minor key.

A ten-minute routine

  1. Two minutes: on one root, play the shells maj7, then 7, then m7, moving one finger at a time.
  2. Three minutes: set the harmonizer to a major key and play its seven seventh chords as shells.
  3. Three minutes: in one minor key, play i, iv and V7, then the minor ii – V – i.
  4. Two minutes: take a chord chart for any song and decode five symbols out loud.
